What can your ISP actually see during a Zoom call?

QUICK ANSWER

Your ISP can see that you're on a video call, roughly how long it lasts, how much data it uses, and which Zoom server your device is talking to, all from analyzing traffic patterns and connection metadata, without ever needing to decrypt anything. What they generally cannot see, because Zoom encrypts it, is the actual audio, video, or shared screen content.

This is worth being precise about, because “can my ISP see my Zoom call” gets answered too loosely, either with an alarmist “they see everything” or a dismissive “they see nothing,” and neither is quite right.

Zoom encrypts the content of your calls, audio, video, and anything shared on screen, using its own transport encryption. Your ISP sitting between your device and Zoom’s servers can’t decrypt and read that content.

What your ISP can see sits at a different layer: metadata, meaning information about the connection rather than what’s inside it. This includes the IP addresses your device is communicating with (revealing you’re connected to Zoom’s infrastructure, since its server ranges are identifiable), the volume and timing pattern of the data flowing back and forth (video calls have a distinctive, steady, bidirectional pattern quite different from browsing or downloading a file), and the connection’s duration. None of this requires decrypting anything, it’s visible from the connection’s outer envelope, the same way a postal worker can see a package’s size and weight without opening it.

In practice, your ISP knows you were on a video call, roughly when, how long, and how much data it used, genuinely useful for network management, and also more visibility into your habits than “my calls are encrypted” implies. It does not mean they know what you discussed, who else was on the call, or what was on your screen.

Is ISP throttling of video calls actually real, or an urban legend?

QUICK ANSWER

Application-specific throttling is a real, documented practice in parts of the telecom industry, though its prevalence varies significantly by country, ISP, and network conditions, it isn't a universal conspiracy, but it also isn't a myth invented by VPN marketing departments.

This deserves a genuinely honest answer, since it gets exaggerated in both directions depending on who’s telling the story.

Some VPN marketing implies that every ISP is constantly throttling every video call, which isn’t accurate, most home broadband in developed markets has enough capacity that conferencing traffic doesn’t get singled out under normal conditions. But dismissing the concern entirely ignores real, documented cases: some mobile carriers have historically throttled or deprioritized video streaming traffic during network congestion, identifiable exactly because of the distinctive traffic pattern described above, the ISP doesn’t need to know it’s Zoom specifically, just that it looks like real-time video.

The more common, better-documented scenario isn’t “my ISP hates Zoom”, it’s congestion-based deprioritization, where an ISP’s network management applies lower priority to traffic patterns identified as streaming or video conferencing during periods of high load. This is more common on mobile data and lower-tier broadband than on high-end fiber, and more common during peak evening hours than at other times.

A VPN’s relevance here is specific: because it encrypts your traffic and routes it to a VPN server rather than directly to Zoom’s infrastructure, your ISP sees an encrypted tunnel to one destination rather than a traffic pattern it can categorize as “video call.” This can, where an ISP’s traffic-shaping specifically targets identifiable conferencing patterns, avoid that deprioritization. It’s not a guarantee, a VPN’s own traffic can be identified as encrypted-tunnel traffic and treated according to the ISP’s broader VPN policy, but for the specific practice of application-pattern-based shaping, routing through a VPN genuinely does sidestep it.

Why does latency matter more than speed for video calls?

QUICK ANSWER

Video calls are real-time, two-way, continuous data streams, which makes them far more sensitive to latency (delay) and jitter (inconsistency in that delay) than to raw bandwidth, a fast connection with high or unstable latency will produce a worse call experience than a slower connection with low, stable latency.

This is genuinely one of the more counterintuitive parts of video-call quality, and it’s worth explaining clearly, since most consumer speed marketing focuses on the wrong number.

Downloading a large file is tolerant of latency: it doesn’t matter much whether each piece of data takes 20 milliseconds or 200 milliseconds to arrive, since the file just finishes a bit sooner or later and you don’t perceive the difference. A video call is the opposite: it’s a continuous, real-time, two-way stream, where each small chunk of audio and video needs to arrive within a tight window to play back smoothly. High latency shows up as a noticeable delay between when someone speaks and when you hear it, the awkward “wait, no, you go” familiar from bad international calls. Jitter, inconsistency in that delay, is often worse, causing audio to stutter and video to freeze even when average latency looks fine on paper.

A VPN’s routing choices affect latency and jitter directly, since your traffic now detours through the VPN server before reaching Zoom’s infrastructure. A server that’s physically far away adds real, physical latency, data genuinely takes longer to travel further, an unavoidable consequence of distance and cabling, not something engineering can fully overcome. A server overloaded with too many simultaneous users can introduce processing delay and inconsistency, showing up as jitter rather than a straightforward drop in speed.

This is exactly why we recommend a server geographically close to you for video calls, even if a more distant one tests faster on a raw speed test, that number simply isn’t measuring what determines whether your call sounds smooth or stutters. A ping test to your chosen server, alongside Zoom’s own built-in connection-quality indicator, gives a far more useful picture than a generic speed test.

Why does post-quantum-ready encryption matter for meetings specifically?

QUICK ANSWER

Even though a single Zoom call feels ephemeral, the connection metadata a VPN's handshake protects, who you're communicating with, when, and for how long, can remain sensitive for years, making the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat directly relevant even to something as seemingly disposable as a routine meeting.

It’s worth grounding this in something concrete, since “why would anyone care about quantum computers and my Tuesday standup” is a fair reaction on its face.

The content of a specific Zoom call is rarely sensitive years down the line, most meetings aren’t secret in the way a classified document is. What can stay sensitive is the pattern of who you were talking to, and when: a series of calls with a specific counterpart during a business negotiation, a recurring pattern of calls with a legal or medical professional, or a call schedule that reveals something about your work, health, or relationships you’d rather not have reconstructed later. This is exactly the metadata a VPN’s tunnel protects from your ISP, and exactly what’s relevant to the “harvest now, decrypt later” threat: an adversary records your encrypted traffic today, without being able to read it, and stores it hoping to decrypt it once a sufficiently powerful quantum computer exists, mainstream estimates place that between the early 2030s and mid-2040s.

The vulnerable part of a VPN connection isn’t the bulk encryption of the call’s audio and video, that holds up reasonably well even against quantum attacks, it’s the handshake, where your device and the server agree on a shared secret using classical elliptic-curve math a capable quantum computer is expected to eventually break. OllaVPN addresses this with a hybrid handshake combining a classical algorithm with a newer, quantum-resistant one based on NIST’s ML-KEM standard, meaning an attacker would need to break both to compromise the connection.

This costs a few extra milliseconds during setup, not a noticeable amount of latency once the call is running, while meaningfully extending how long your calling metadata stays protected.

How does a “no-logs” claim actually get verified?

QUICK ANSWER

A no-logs claim is best verified through independent third-party security audits, a specific and named list of what isn't collected, and, where possible, direct technical testing from your own device, rather than by trusting the marketing copy alone.

Look for a specific, itemized policy, not a vague sentence. A meaningful no-logs policy names exactly what isn’t retained: connection timestamps tied to identity, IP address logs, bandwidth usage tied to an individual account, and DNS query logs.

Test what you can from your own device. You can’t directly verify “we don’t log your connection metadata,” but you can verify related technical claims, whether your DNS requests actually route through the provider’s own resolver rather than leaking to your network, and whether your real IP is exposed anywhere.
