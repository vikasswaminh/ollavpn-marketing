How Do Their Server Networks Compare?

QUICK ANSWER

The number of servers and their distribution vary widely across VPNs, affecting speed, reliability, and access to specific locations.

The number of servers and their distribution vary widely across VPNs, affecting speed, reliability, and access to specific locations.

Most VPNs boast about their "number of servers," implying more is always better. While a larger overall server count can indicate a robust network, it's not the only factor. What matters more for you is the \*\*country distribution\*\*, do they have servers in the locations you need? Some VPNs might have thousands of servers, but if they're all concentrated in just a few countries, they might not be useful for accessing content from a specific region you're interested in. For OllaVPN, we focus on providing access to every country in our network, ensuring you're not limited.

Another crucial distinction is between \*\*physical vs. virtual servers\*\*. Physical servers are actual hardware located in the country they appear to be in. Virtual servers, on the other hand, are configured to appear in a specific country but are physically located elsewhere. While virtual servers can offer flexibility, they can sometimes lead to higher latency if the physical server is far away. Many VPNs use a mix, and it's not always transparent which is which. We aim for transparency in our network, prioritizing performance and privacy.

Beyond just the count or type, the way a VPN manages its network affects your experience. A well-maintained network with up-to-date hardware and efficient routing protocols will generally perform better, even if it has a slightly lower "number of servers" than a competitor. This also impacts the reliability and consistency of your connection. A vetting checklist for any VPN comparison you read

QUICK ANSWER

Not every "X vs Y" article on the internet uses a defensible methodology. When you read someone else's comparison, watch for these patterns:

Not every "X vs Y" article on the internet uses a defensible methodology. When you read someone else's comparison, watch for these patterns:

**Affiliate-driven rankings.** Many comparison sites are funded by VPN affiliate commissions. Operators that pay higher commissions tend to win those comparisons. The tell is rankings that don't match the actual technical merits or audit history, and "best for streaming" being the default lead category, because streaming-VPN affiliate payouts are highest.

**Stale data.** VPN operators change pricing, ownership, jurisdiction, and feature sets continuously. A comparison written in 2023 and republished in 2026 without re-checking is unreliable. Look for a "last updated" date and a methodology section before trusting the comparison.

**Missing audit attribution.** Trustworthy comparisons cite the audit firm and year for each operator's last independent audit. If a "no-logs" claim is repeated without saying who verified it and when, the claim is doing less work than it appears to.

**No coverage of what each operator does NOT do.** Honest comparisons include the limitations and non-features of each operator. A comparison that only lists positives is selling, not informing.
