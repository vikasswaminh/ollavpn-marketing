Why Is Public Wi-Fi Abroad Actually Risky?

Public Wi-Fi abroad is risky because you have no way to verify who runs the network, how it’s configured, or who else is on it, meaning your traffic can be intercepted, your device can be probed by others on the same segment, and fake networks can impersonate real ones. At home, you probably trust your café or gym’s Wi-Fi because you’ve used it dozens of times without incident. Abroad, you have zero history with any network. You’re trusting a name on a splash screen, “Hotel_Guest,” “Free_Airport_WiFi”, that could be exactly what it claims, or a laptop in someone’s backpack broadcasting an identical name to lure your phone in. This is common enough to have a name: the evil twin attack. Your phone doesn’t verify that “Hotel_Guest” is the router bolted to the lobby ceiling; it just sees a familiar name and connects. Once you’re on an attacker-controlled network, they can watch unencrypted traffic and redirect you to fake login pages that look identical to your bank. Even on a legitimate network, encryption between your device and the router is often weaker than assumed. Many public hotspots still use a shared password printed at reception, meaning every guest has the same key, which isn’t much of a secret when 200 people know it. Some networks skip device-level encryption entirely and rely only on a login splash page, so traffic is broadcast in the clear until it reaches the router. Then there’s simple observation, which needs no sophistication at all. Unencrypted HTTP traffic, DNS queries, and connection metadata are visible to anyone with a laptop and free packet-sniffing software running nearby. You don’t need to be a nation-state actor to watch which domains a phone two tables away is quietly resolving. Bottom line: A VPN addresses the part of this that’s actually solvable from your side. It encrypts everything leaving your device before it touches the local Wi-Fi, so it doesn’t matter whether the hotel’s encryption is strong or nonexistent, and it doesn’t matter whether the network is real or an evil twin sitting three feet away, the operator only sees encrypted traffic, never your logins, messages, or browsing.

What’s Really Happening on Hotel Wi-Fi?

Hotel Wi-Fi typically runs on flat, shared networks where every guest is one hop from every other guest, router firmware is rarely updated, and the property has full visibility into every domain you visit even when your traffic is otherwise encrypted. Most hotel networks, especially outside major chains with dedicated IT budgets, are built cheaply and left alone for years. The router in the utility closet was installed when the hotel opened and hasn’t been patched since; the login portal exists mainly to segment paying guests, not to protect you from the guest in room 412. Researchers have repeatedly found outdated firmware and default credentials still active on hotel network gear, a documented, recurring finding. The bigger issue is topology. A well-run network isolates devices from each other; hotel networks often skip this because it’s cheaper to put everyone on one flat subnet. A moderately curious guest with basic tools can sometimes see other devices on the same segment, and in poorly configured setups, even reach open ports on other guests’ laptops. Then there’s the hotel as an observer. Even when device-to-router encryption is reasonable, the hotel’s infrastructure still sees metadata, which sites you visit, when, and how much data you move. Hotel networks are increasingly monetized: some properties partner with analytics companies that build guest profiles from Wi-Fi usage, sold onward as aggregated data, buried in terms you tapped “accept” on at check-in. Bottom line: A VPN moves the point where your traffic becomes visible from “the hotel’s router” to “your own device,” so the hotel network, however old, flat, or monetized, sees only an encrypted tunnel, not your destinations or content.

What’s Different About Airport Wi-Fi?

Airport Wi-Fi carries a distinct risk because it attracts stressed, distracted travelers doing sensitive things quickly, checking in, paying for lounge access, handling boarding passes, on networks that are frequently free, unauthenticated, and specifically targeted because of that behavior. Think about your own airport habits: rushing, half-watching a departure board, connecting to Wi-Fi to check a gate change or transfer money before a flight. Attackers exploit exactly this. Honeypot experiments at major airports have repeatedly shown that a fake network with a plausible name, “Airport_Free_WiFi”, picks up dozens to hundreds of connections within hours, simply because it looks like what people expect. Airports also have a density problem. A single international terminal can see tens of thousands of devices connecting across a day, funneled through a handful of access points. That density makes passive interception efficient: set up once and capture a continuous stream of travelers doing time-sensitive, often financially meaningful things, last-minute ticket purchases, connection check-ins, parking payments, corporate email during a delay. Genuine airport Wi-Fi, run by the airport authority, is often reasonably maintained because airports know they’re a target. The problem is you generally can’t tell a real network from a spoofed one just by its name, both can look identical, and captive login pages can be faked just as convincingly. Bottom line: Connect the VPN the moment you join any airport network, before doing anything that matters. Whether the access point is legitimate or a laptop in someone’s carry-on, your session stays unreadable to whoever’s on the other end.

Is Roaming Data Safer Than Public Wi-Fi?

Roaming mobile data is generally more resistant to local eavesdropping than open Wi-Fi because cellular traffic is encrypted between your device and the tower, but it isn’t private from your carrier or the foreign network you’re roaming onto, and it can be slow, capped, or expensive. The link between your phone and the cell tower uses encryption built into the mobile protocol itself, a meaningfully higher baseline than an open hotspot with no encryption or a password known to hundreds of guests. There’s no shared local network for a nearby device to snoop on. That said, “safer than open Wi-Fi” is a low bar. When you roam, your data typically routes through a partner network abroad, and that local operator, along with your home carrier, has visibility into connection metadata, which domains you resolve, how much data you use, and in countries with data retention laws, records kept for months or years and available to authorities on request. There’s also a cost problem unrelated to privacy: even generous “unlimited” roaming plans often throttle heavily after a threshold, and pay-as-you-go rates in some countries can produce a surprising bill. Many travelers solve this with a local prepaid SIM or eSIM, smart for cost, but it introduces a new, unknown operator whose logging practices you likely haven’t researched. Bottom line: A VPN doesn’t change your bill or speed, but it changes what’s visible in between. Whether roaming on your carrier’s partner network or a fresh local SIM, the tunnel means the mobile operator sees encrypted traffic to a VPN server, not a readable list of everything you touched during your trip.

What Mistakes Do Travelers Make With Free VPNs?

The most common mistake is downloading an unfamiliar free VPN for the first time only after arriving somewhere, without checking its funding model, logging policy, or reviews, often at exactly the moment travelers are least equipped to evaluate any of it. Picture the scenario: you land, the hotel Wi-Fi needs a password you don’t have yet, you remember you meant to get a VPN, and you search “free VPN” right there in the lobby. There’s also a mistake in the other direction: assuming a short trip isn’t worth the setup and skipping a VPN entirely. Bottom line: Sort out your VPN before you travel, on a stable connection, with time to actually read what you’re installing, rather than under the exact conditions that make careful evaluation hardest.

Are VPNs Legal Everywhere You’re Going?

VPN use is legal in the vast majority of countries travelers visit, but a small number restrict, heavily regulate, or ban VPN use outright, so it’s worth checking your specific destination before relying on one. Most destinations, the EU, UK, US, Canada, Japan, South Korea, Australia, most of Latin America, and most of Southeast Asia, treat VPN use as ordinary and legal, no different from any other software. Using a VPN on hotel or airport Wi-Fi in these places is simply a privacy tool, not a legal Gray area. A handful of countries take a different approach, generally as part of broader internet censorship rather than a specific objection to VPNs. China restricts VPN use to a small number of officially licensed services, and unauthorized apps are frequently blocked at the network level. Russia, the UAE, Iran, and a few others have varying restrictions, with penalties ranging from fines to more serious consequences. If your itinerary includes any of these countries, research the current legal status before you travel, since rules change, and understand that even where VPN use isn’t explicitly illegal, some governments throttle or block known server IP ranges, meaning the experience can be unreliable even when the legal risk is manageable. For the overwhelming majority of trips, a conference in Berlin, a holiday in Thailand, a work trip to Toronto, none of this is a live concern, and a VPN is simply a sensible, unremarkable privacy tool.

Why Does Post-Quantum-Ready Encryption Matter on the Road?

Post-quantum-ready encryption matters while traveling because your trip data: logins, messages, financial details entered on unfamiliar networks: could be captured today by an attacker on a shady hotel or airport network, then decrypted years later once quantum computers become powerful enough to break today’s standard encryption.

This is sometimes called “harvest now, decrypt later,” and it’s especially relevant on travel networks, which are exactly the higher-risk environments where traffic capture is more plausible than on your secured home network. An attacker on a compromised hotel router or fake airport hotspot doesn’t need to break your encryption today, they just record it and wait. Given that quantum computing capable of breaking current public-key standards is an area of active research, data captured on this year’s trip could theoretically become readable in the coming years without quantum-resistant protection. OllaVPN protects against this with a hybrid handshake pairing a well-established classical algorithm with a newer, quantum-resistant one. Both have to fail for your connection to be broken, so even if quantum computers eventually crack one, the connection remains protected by the other. It’s forward-looking protection for exactly the kind of data you handle while traveling: banking logins, work correspondence, personal messages, identity documents you photograph and send to yourself. The practical takeaway: this extra layer costs you nothing in convenience or speed on the free tier, you get it by default, on every connection, everywhere in the network.
