Do you really need a VPN for public Wi-Fi?

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, absolutely, you should always use a VPN on public Wi-Fi.

Yes, absolutely, you should always use a VPN on public Wi-Fi.

Public Wi-Fi, whether it's at a coffee shop, airport, or hotel, is convenient but rarely secure. Think of it like shouting your private conversations across a crowded room, anyone can listen in. These are often **unsecured networks**, meaning the connection between your device and the internet is wide open. This leaves your data vulnerable to interception by anyone else on the same network, which is a much bigger risk than you might think. Without a VPN, every bit of data you send or receive, from your browsing history to your login credentials for online banking, could be exposed. Criminals use various techniques, like "man-in-the-middle" attacks, to snoop on your traffic, steal sensitive information, or even inject malware onto your device. It’s a bit like having a locked front door but leaving all your windows wide open; a VPN closes those windows. A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and the internet. This tunnel scrambles your data, making it unreadable to anyone who might try to intercept it on an unsecured network. Even if someone manages to grab your data, all they'll see is gibberish. This protection extends to everything you do online, giving you peace of mind whether you're checking email, shopping, or just browsing. Given the risks, there's really no reason not to use a VPN. What are the actual threats on public Wi-Fi?

QUICK ANSWER

On public Wi-Fi, the main threats are snooping, data theft through fake networks, and attackers intercepting your connection to steal sensitive information.

On public Wi-Fi, the main threats are snooping, data theft through fake networks, and attackers intercepting your connection to steal sensitive information.

When you connect to public Wi-Fi, you're essentially joining a network where anyone else can be lurking. Think of it like shouting your conversations in a crowded room, everyone can potentially hear you. The biggest danger here is that your data isn't always encrypted between your device and the Wi-Fi router. This means if you're sending any kind of sensitive information, like your **login credentials** for an online banking site or even just browsing certain unencrypted websites, it can be easily intercepted. An attacker doesn't even need to be particularly sophisticated; they just need some basic tools to perform what's called **packet sniffing**, where they literally "sniff" out data packets traveling across the network. Another major threat comes from **Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attacks**. This is where an attacker inserts themselves between your device and the internet, often by setting up a fake Wi-Fi hotspot that looks legitimate (like "Free Airport Wi-Fi"). When you connect to their fake network, all your traffic flows through their device first. They can then see everything you do, modify the data you send or receive, and even redirect you to malicious websites without you ever knowing. They can steal passwords, read your emails, or inject malware onto your device. Even on a legitimate public Wi-Fi network, **other users on the network** can pose a risk. If your device settings aren't hardened (for example, if you have file sharing enabled or certain ports open), a malicious user on the same network could try to access your device directly. While this is less common with modern operating systems having better defaults, it's still a vector. Essentially, public Wi-Fi is a shared space, and without your own protective measures, you're relying entirely on the security practices of the café, airport, or hotel providing the connection, which are often not up to par. Connecting with a VPN like OllaVPN encrypts your traffic from your device all the way to our servers, making it unreadable to anyone else on that public network.

What can’t a VPN protect you from on public Wi-Fi?

QUICK ANSWER

A VPN can't protect you from malware, phishing scams, or social engineering attacks, as these don't rely on intercepting your network traffic.

A VPN can't protect you from malware, phishing scams, or social engineering attacks, as these don't rely on intercepting your network traffic.

A VPN is fantastic for encrypting your online activity and masking your IP address, especially on unsecured public Wi-Fi. It creates a secure tunnel between your device and the internet, making it very difficult for anyone on the same network to snoop on your data. But it's not a magic bullet for every digital threat. Think of it like a bulletproof vest: it protects your torso, but it doesn't make you invulnerable to everything. For instance, a VPN won't stop you from downloading a malicious file or clicking on a shady link in a phishing email. These are threats that originate from the content you interact with, not the network connection itself. If you're tricked into giving away your login details by a convincing fake website, your VPN won't prevent that data from going to the wrong hands. That falls under **social engineering**, where attackers exploit human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities. To truly stay safe online, you need a multi-layered approach. Always be wary of unsolicited emails and messages, and verify the sender before clicking any links. Keep your software updated to patch known vulnerabilities that malware might exploit. Using **strong, unique passwords** for all your accounts and enabling two-factor authentication adds another crucial layer of protection. Finally, pay attention to your **browser security** settings and consider using browser extensions that block trackers and malicious sites. A VPN is a powerful tool, but it works best when combined with good digital hygiene.

What a VPN actually does (and doesn’t do) for this use case

QUICK ANSWER

VPN marketing tends to overpromise on specific use cases. The honest framing for the scenario above:

VPN marketing tends to overpromise on specific use cases. The honest framing for the scenario above:

**What the VPN actually changes.** Your apparent IP address (the destination sees the exit's IP, not yours). Your ISP's visibility into your destinations and DNS lookups (your ISP sees you're using a VPN; not what's inside). Whether other devices on the local network can sniff your traffic (they cannot). The presence or absence of encryption end-to-end depends on what the destination supports, HTTPS handles encryption between you and the destination; the VPN handles encryption between you and the exit.

**What the VPN doesn't change.** Whether the destination service can fingerprint your browser. Whether your account login identifies you to the service you're logging into. Whether your installed apps phone home with telemetry. Whether your operating system itself has compromised components. The VPN is one privacy layer; it doesn't replace browser hardening, account hygiene, or endpoint security.

**What success looks like in this use case.** For most use cases that fit the scenario above, success means: the third party you're trying to hide from cannot see what you're doing. If that third party is your ISP, the VPN is fully sufficient. If that third party is the destination website (e.g., for evading detection), the VPN helps but the destination has many other detection methods (browser fingerprint, account, device ID) that the VPN doesn't address.

When a VPN is not the right tool

QUICK ANSWER

Honest recommendations for this use case include knowing when to reach for something other than a VPN:

Honest recommendations for this use case include knowing when to reach for something other than a VPN:

**If the goal is anonymity in the strict sense** (no observer, not even a global passive adversary, can identify the source of traffic), Tor is the right tool. It is materially slower than any VPN, but the source-anonymity property is meaningfully different from what a VPN provides. Use Tor for that specific use case and accept the latency.

**If the goal is bypassing extreme state-level censorship** (Iran's Great Firewall equivalent, China's Great Firewall), specialised tools like Psiphon, Lantern, and Tor bridges are built for that exact use case and invest heavily in obfuscation. Consumer VPNs work some days and not others in those environments; specialised tools work consistently.

**If the goal is protecting devices on your home network from each other** (a compromised IoT camera attacking your laptop), a VPN doesn't address that, segmentation at the local-network level (VLANs, a separate IoT Wi-Fi network) is the right tool.

**If the goal is blocking ads and trackers**, a VPN doesn't do this by itself (though OllaVPN's planned Pro-tier resolver-level blocker will help, and uBlock Origin in the browser already does most of the work today). VPN + ad blocker + privacy-respecting browser is the layered approach.
