Who wins in terms of speed and protocols?

ExpressVPN generally has a slight edge in speed and consistency over many providers due to its proprietary Lightway protocol, while free VPNs often fall short.

ExpressVPN developed its own protocol called **Lightway protocol (ExpressVPN)**, which is designed to be lightweight, fast, and secure. This often gives it a real advantage, particularly on mobile devices or unstable networks, because it can maintain speed consistency better than some other options. What's their privacy posture and audit history like?

Different VPNs have different approaches to privacy and auditing, ranging from transparent no-logs policies in privacy-friendly jurisdictions to less clear stances without independent verification.

When you're looking at a VPN, their privacy posture is probably one of the most important things to consider. It's not just about what they say they do, but what they actually *can* do, and what they've proven they do. Many VPNs claim a "no-logs policy," which sounds great, but you need to dig a little deeper. Does that mean no connection logs? No activity logs? Do they collect your IP address when you sign up? And perhaps most importantly, how do they prove any of this? A big part of a VPN's privacy chops comes down to its **jurisdiction**. Some countries are known for strong privacy laws, while others have mandatory data retention, or participate in intelligence-sharing alliances. For example, some VPNs might be based in privacy-friendly locales like the British Virgin Islands, which generally means they're under no legal obligation to log user data or share it with governments. Others might be in countries like the Netherlands, which, while generally good on privacy, can have different legal pressures. It's a complex picture, and where a company is legally incorporated can have a real impact on what they're compelled to do. Beyond jurisdiction, **independent security audits** are crucial. A VPN can say they have a no-logs policy all day long, but without an external, unbiased third party verifying those claims, it's just words. These audits typically examine their infrastructure, code, and internal processes to ensure they align with their public statements. A comprehensive audit will cover everything from server configurations to how they handle user data and whether their no-logs policy is genuinely enforceable. Without this, you're essentially taking their word for it. At OllaVPN, we've built our service around a no-logs, no-email, no-card free tier. We don't ask for your email address, and you certainly don't need a credit card. This means there's no personal identifier tied to your free account from the start. We're funded entirely by our optional Plus subscribers, so we have no incentive to sell your data or show you ads. This model, combined with our technical architecture, helps ensure that your privacy isn't just a policy, but a fundamental part of how we operate.

How we evaluated these — methodology disclosure

A head-to-head comparison is only as honest as the methodology behind it. For every comparison we publish, the framework is the same:

Data sources. Each operator's publicly documented privacy policy (read fully, not skimmed); their published independent audit reports where available (Securitum, Cure53, Radically Open Security, and KPMG have all audited VPNs in the names we compare); their security disclosure pages and warrant canaries; their pricing pages at the time of writing; and our own hands-on testing on at least two of Windows, macOS, and Android during the most recent quarterly refresh.

What we test. DNS-leak posture (using our DNS lookup tool + dnsleaktest.com cross-check), WebRTC leak posture via our test, kill-switch behavior under simulated network changes (toggling Wi-Fi off mid-session, putting the laptop to sleep with VPN active, swapping Wi-Fi networks), and IPv6 leak posture.

What we do not test. Long-term performance under heavy load (we lack multi-month data on competitors), specific streaming-service compatibility (rotates daily, unfair to compare), and per-region speed (depends on the user's ISP and exit choice, not the VPN's design).

Conflict-of-interest disclosure. OllaVPN is one of the operators we compare on this site. We manage the conflict by being explicit about methodology, by ranking by best-fit-for-the-reader's-situation rather than by what makes us look best, and by accepting that some readers will pick a different operator. The reader who picks Proton VPN, Mullvad, or another name after reading our comparison is a reader we still consider a good outcome.

A vetting checklist for any VPN comparison you read

Not every "X vs Y" article on the internet uses a defensible methodology. When you read someone else's comparison, watch for these patterns:

Affiliate-driven rankings. Many comparison sites are funded by VPN affiliate commissions. Operators that pay higher commissions tend to win those comparisons. The tell is rankings that don't match the actual technical merits or audit history — and "best for streaming" being the default lead category, because streaming-VPN affiliate payouts are highest.

Stale data. VPN operators change pricing, ownership, jurisdiction, and feature sets continuously. A comparison written in 2023 and republished in 2026 without re-checking is unreliable. Look for a "last updated" date and a methodology section before trusting the comparison.

Missing audit attribution. Trustworthy comparisons cite the audit firm and year for each operator's last independent audit. If a "no-logs" claim is repeated without saying who verified it and when, the claim is doing less work than it appears to.

No coverage of what each operator does NOT do. Honest comparisons include the limitations and non-features of each operator. A comparison that only lists positives is selling, not informing.
