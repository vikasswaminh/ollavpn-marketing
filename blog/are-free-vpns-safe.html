What does a 'free VPN' actually mean, and what doesn't it?

"Free VPN" can mean a few different things, from truly free services funded by other means, to ad-supported models, or services that collect and sell your data.

When you see "free VPN," it usually falls into one of three categories, and it's super important to know the difference. The first kind is the ad-funded free VPNs. These VPNs might not charge you money, but they make their cash by showing you ads, usually within the app itself. Sometimes, it's more subtle, like injecting ads into the websites you visit, which is a big privacy red flag. It means someone's looking at your traffic to decide which ads to show, and that's not what you want from a privacy tool. Then there are the freemium throttled VPNs. These are very common. They give you a basic, free version of their service, but it's often limited by data caps, speed restrictions, or a small selection of server locations. Their goal is to get you hooked and then convince you to upgrade to a paid "premium" plan for full access. There's nothing inherently wrong with this model, as long as they're transparent about their free tier's limitations and don't resort to shady practices to make money. Finally, you have what we call honest-loss-leader free VPNs. This is where OllaVPN fits in. Our free tier is genuinely free forever: no card, no email tracking, no ads, no data sale, and no logs. We fund it entirely through our Plus subscribers, not by monetizing your data or showing you ads. We cap your speed at 10 Mbps on the free plan, but you get access to every country in our network, with no data ceiling. If you need more speed or want to use it on more devices, you can upgrade to OllaVPN Plus for a small monthly fee. This way, we keep the free tier truly private and sustainable without compromising your security or privacy.

What should you expect from any honest free VPN in this category?

You should expect transparent policies, strong encryption, a strict no-logs policy, and essential security features like a kill switch.

What you should expect from any honest free VPN in this category really boils down to trust and basic competence. First, they should be upfront about how they fund their free service. If they're not charging you, they're getting money somewhere, and that needs to be crystal clear. For us, it's our Plus subscribers. For others, it might be ads or a data cap. The key is transparency. Beyond the business model, a privacy-first VPN, free or paid, absolutely needs to offer robust encryption. This means using modern, proven protocols like WireGuard, not outdated or easily-broken ones. Your data needs to be scrambled effectively between your device and the VPN server, protecting you from snoopers and your internet provider. If a free VPN isn't using strong encryption, it's not really doing its job. Crucially, you should demand a clear and verifiable no-logs policy. This means the VPN provider isn't keeping records of your online activity – what websites you visit, what files you download, or your IP address. If they log your activity, they could potentially hand it over to authorities or sell it, completely defeating the purpose of a VPN. Finally, essential security features like an automatic kill switch are non-negotiable. This feature cuts your internet connection if the VPN unexpectedly drops, preventing your real IP address from being exposed.

Why this topic matters beyond the immediate question

The topic above sits at the intersection of several broader concerns that are worth understanding even if your immediate question has been answered:

The privacy-tooling category has matured rapidly. What was leading-edge in 2020 is table stakes in 2026. Standards have finalized (NIST PQC, RFC 9116 security disclosure, the IETF MASQUE work), jurisdictions have clarified (post-CERT-In, post-DSA, post-Investigatory Powers Act), and a clear trustworthy/untrustworthy line has emerged in the consumer VPN category specifically. Decisions you make today should reflect the 2026 state of the market, not the 2020 state.

The threat landscape has shifted. Bulk traffic capture by intelligence services is well-documented and continuous. The "harvest now, decrypt later" threat against captured-today-decrypted-tomorrow ciphertext is operational, not theoretical. ISP-level metadata sales to advertising data brokers are routine in the US. Geo-blocking and censorship are both more sophisticated and more widespread. The tools that defended you adequately five years ago may not be adequate now.

The user has more power than ever — if they use it. Strong free privacy tools exist in every category. The question is whether the user knows about them, knows how to verify they actually work, and is willing to take the small additional steps required. We try to make the verification path as short as possible because users who can verify are users who can choose well.
