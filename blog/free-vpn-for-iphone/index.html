What does “no credit card, no logs” actually mean?

QUICK ANSWER

“No credit card” means you can download, install, and start using the VPN without entering payment details - not a free trial that quietly converts to a paid subscription.

“No logs” means the provider doesn’t record your browsing activity, connection timestamps, or the websites you visit in a way that could later identify you.

These two phrases get used loosely enough in App Store listings that it’s worth pinning them down. The gap between what they should mean and what they sometimes mean is exactly where people get burned.

“No credit card” should mean there is no payment method on file at all - not a card charged $0.00 today that starts billing after a trial period ends. This is a common pattern in the App Store VPN category: a “free” download that requires you to start a subscription through Apple’s in-app purchase system, with small-print billing that begins automatically after 3 or 7 days unless you cancel. That’s not a free VPN. It’s a trial with an automatic-renewal trap - and it depends on people forgetting to cancel.

“No logs” should mean the provider doesn’t store information that could reconstruct your browsing activity - no record of which websites you visited, no timestamped connection logs tied to your identity, no retained history of your traffic.

The catch is that “no logs” is one of the most abused phrases in VPN marketing. A provider can technically be telling the truth about not logging your browsing history while still logging connection timestamps, device identifiers, or approximate location - details that, together, can still identify you. A meaningful no-logs claim should be specific about what isn’t stored, and ideally backed by an independent audit.

Put together, “no credit card, no logs” is a promise that the product is free at the point of use and free of the surveillance that funds a lot of “free” software. It’s a high bar, and most listings that use the phrase don’t actually clear it.

Regular updates. Networking edge cases and iOS platform changes get discovered and patched over time. A VPN app that hasn’t been updated in a year on the App Store is a warning sign, regardless of its other qualities.

A real, native iOS app - not a configuration profile you have to manually install, and not a browser-only workaround. Apple’s Network Extension framework is how legitimate VPN apps integrate properly, including features like an on-device kill switch.

Why does post-quantum-ready encryption matter on a phone specifically?

QUICK ANSWER

Your iPhone carries an unusually concentrated, long-lived record of sensitive personal data - messages, health information, financial logins, location history - much of which needs to stay confidential for years or decades. Post-quantum-ready encryption protects the VPN handshake securing that data against future quantum decryption of traffic recorded today.

It’s worth explaining why this matters more on a phone than people often assume, instead of treating it as a generic buzzword.

Your iPhone is arguably the single most data-dense device most people own. It holds years of private messages, health and fitness data, financial app logins, location history, and photos - often synced continuously across services. A meaningful share of that data needs to remain private for a long time, not just today, but in five, ten, or twenty years.

That’s exactly the timeframe relevant to the “harvest now, decrypt later” threat. An adversary can record your encrypted VPN traffic today without being able to read it, store that recording, and attempt to decrypt it once a sufficiently powerful quantum computer exists. Mainstream estimates place that somewhere between the early 2030s and the mid-2040s.

The part of a VPN connection actually vulnerable to this isn’t the bulk encryption of your traffic - that’s handled by AES-256, which holds up well even against quantum attacks. The vulnerable piece is the handshake, the initial step where your phone and the VPN server agree on a shared secret key using public-key cryptography. Classical elliptic-curve math currently protects that step, but a capable quantum computer is expected to eventually break it.

OllaVPN addresses this with a hybrid handshake that combines a classical, well-established algorithm with a newer, quantum-resistant one (based on NIST’s standardized ML-KEM algorithm). Because both algorithms run side by side and are combined into the final secret, an attacker would need to break both - not just one - to compromise your connection.

If the post-quantum component ever turned out to have an undiscovered flaw, the classical component still protects you against classical attackers. If a future quantum computer eventually breaks the classical component, the post-quantum component still holds. It’s a hedge in both directions, and it costs only a few extra milliseconds during connection setup - nothing you’ll notice as ongoing battery drain or speed loss.

Given how much of your life genuinely lives on your iPhone, this is one case where “future-proofing” is a pretty literal description of what’s happening, not a marketing flourish.

How does a “no-logs” claim actually get verified?

QUICK ANSWER

A no-logs claim is best verified through independent third-party security audits, a specific and named list of what isn’t collected, and - where possible - direct technical testing from your own device, rather than trusting marketing copy alone.

This is worth addressing directly, because “no logs” is a claim, not a fact you should take on faith from any provider - including us. Here’s how to actually check it.

Look for a specific, itemized policy, not a vague sentence. A meaningful no-logs policy names exactly what categories of data aren’t retained: browsing history, DNS queries, connection timestamps tied to identity, IP address logs, and bandwidth usage tied to an individual account. Vague reassurance without a specific list is a weaker signal.

Look for an independent audit. Reputable VPN providers periodically commission security firms - names like Cure53, Securitum, or KPMG show up often in this space - to review their infrastructure and confirm the no-logs claim holds up against the actual server configuration, not just the written policy. A clean, recent, published audit is one of the strongest trust signals available. The complete absence of any audit history - especially when competitors have one - is worth noticing.

Test what you can from your own device. You can’t verify “we don’t log your browsing” directly, but you can verify related technical claims: whether your DNS requests are routed through the provider’s own resolver instead of leaking to your carrier, and whether your real IP address is exposed anywhere. Tools like a DNS leak test or a WebRTC leak test, run before and after connecting, show the difference clearly.

Check the company’s jurisdiction and legal history. Some countries have data-retention laws that could compel logging regardless of a provider’s stated policy. A provider based somewhere with strong privacy protections - and with no history of having handed over user data under legal pressure it claimed it couldn’t produce - is a stronger bet than one that’s never been tested.

We publish our own policy specifics rather than vague reassurance. We’d encourage you to apply the same checklist to us, not just to competitors.
