Why is ISP-level throttling on Jio, Airtel, and BSNL a real privacy concern?

ISP-level throttling is a major privacy concern because it reveals that your internet provider is actively inspecting and categorizing your online activities.

It's easy to think of throttling as just a speed problem – your internet slows down, and that's annoying. But the real privacy implication runs much deeper. For an ISP like Jio, Airtel, or BSNL to throttle specific types of traffic, they first have to know what kind of traffic it is. This isn't just about how much data you're using; it's about what you're doing with that data. They're not guessing; they're looking. This "looking" is often done through a technique called Deep Packet Inspection (DPI). DPI allows your ISP to examine the data packets you send and receive, identifying the application, service, or even the content type. If they see you're streaming video, using a voice-over-IP service, or torrenting, they can then decide to slow that specific traffic down. The privacy concern isn't just that they *can* do it, but that they *are* doing it – constantly monitoring and categorizing your online behavior without your explicit consent. When your ISP actively monitors your traffic to implement throttling, it fundamentally undermines your expectation of privacy online. They're building a profile of your digital life, seeing which apps you use most, when you use them, and potentially even what you're saying and doing within those apps if they're not properly encrypted. A VPN prevents this by encrypting all your internet traffic and routing it through a secure server. To your ISP, all they see is encrypted data going to a VPN server, making it impossible for them to identify specific activities for throttling or surveillance.

How does a VPN protect you on airport, railway-station, and cafe Wi-Fi?

A VPN protects you on public Wi-Fi by encrypting all your internet traffic, creating a secure tunnel that shields your data from snoopers and malicious actors.

When you connect to public Wi-Fi in places like airports, railway stations, or cafes, you're often sharing the network with many other people, some of whom might have less-than-honourable intentions. These networks are frequently unsecured or poorly secured, making it easy for cybercriminals to intercept your data. This is where the notorious public Wi-Fi risks come into play, as your browsing activity, login credentials, and personal information can be vulnerable to eavesdropping.

One of the biggest threats on these networks is a man-in-the-middle attack. This is where an attacker positions themselves between your device and the internet, secretly intercepting and even altering communications without you knowing. They can grab your passwords, credit card details, or even inject malware into your device. Without a VPN, your connection is like a postcard sent through the mail – anyone can read it. With a VPN, it's like sending that postcard inside a locked, armoured box.

A VPN solves these problems by creating an encrypted secure tunnel between your device and a VPN server. All your internet traffic – every website you visit, every email you send, every app you use – gets scrambled into unreadable code before it leaves your device. This means even if a hacker manages to intercept your data on that dodgy cafe Wi-Fi, all they'll see is gibberish. Your information remains private, and your online activities stay secure, protecting you from surveillance and data theft.

Why does post-quantum encryption matter specifically when bandwidth is metered?

Post-quantum-ready encryption is crucial for metered bandwidth because it provides long-term security, ensuring your data remains protected for decades without needing costly re-encryption or re-transmission if current standards break.

You might be wondering, what exactly is post-quantum-ready encryption, and why does it matter more when your data plan is metered? It's all about future-proofing. Right now, the encryption that protects almost everything online (banking, emails, VPNs) relies on incredibly complex math problems that even the most powerful supercomputers can't solve in a reasonable timeframe. But quantum computers, once they're powerful enough, could potentially break these traditional encryption methods in minutes.

When that day comes, any data encrypted today that's still sensitive will be vulnerable. This is where post-quantum-ready encryption comes in. It uses new cryptographic algorithms designed to withstand attacks from quantum computers, ensuring your data remains secure not just for now, but for the next 20, 30, or even 50 years. For users with metered bandwidth, especially in regions where data is expensive or limited, this long-term security is a massive win. Imagine if you encrypted a critical document or communication today, and in five years, the old encryption breaks. You'd have to re-encrypt and re-transmit that data, using up your precious bandwidth all over again.

With OllaVPN's post-quantum-ready encryption, you're making a one-time investment in security that lasts. You send your data once, knowing it's protected against future threats without needing to constantly re-evaluate and potentially re-send information. This means less wasted data on re-encryption, more reliable long-term privacy, and peace of mind that your sensitive information, whether it's personal communications or financial details, won't suddenly become exposed years down the line because today’s encryption became obsolete. It's about protecting your privacy and your wallet, ensuring your bandwidth is used efficiently for truly secure communication.

What about India's CERT-In rules and mandatory VPN logging?

India's CERT-In directive (April 2022) requires VPN operators with infrastructure in India to log connection data for 5 years. OllaVPN does not operate exit infrastructure inside India — the directive does not apply to our service the way it applies to operators with Indian POPs, which is why several major VPN brands withdrew their Indian servers in 2022. We route Indian users via our nearest exit (typically Frankfurt) rather than maintaining logging-required Indian infrastructure.

India's Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) issued a directive on 28 April 2022 requiring data centres, virtual private server providers, cloud service providers, and virtual private network service providers to maintain ICT system logs for 180 days and to maintain customer information (name, address, email, phone, validation period, IP allocated, purpose of use) for a period of 5 years. The directive applies to providers with infrastructure inside India.

The privacy implications are stark: a no-logs VPN promise structurally cannot coexist with mandatory connection logging. Several major VPN brands — NordVPN, ExpressVPN, Surfshark, ProtonVPN — publicly withdrew their physical Indian servers in 2022 in direct response, and most now serve Indian users from servers in neighbouring countries (typically Singapore, Hong Kong, or Frankfurt) instead.

OllaVPN's posture: we do not operate physical exit infrastructure inside India. Indian users who connect through OllaVPN route via our nearest available exit (typically Frankfurt; latency is around 110-130 ms from most Indian cities, depending on your ISP's peering). The CERT-In logging directive does not apply to traffic that does not pass through Indian-operated VPN infrastructure. This is the same architectural approach Proton VPN, NordVPN, and Mullvad have all adopted post-2022.

The cost is latency: a Mumbai user connecting to a Frankfurt exit has ~30-40 ms more round-trip time than a Mumbai user would have connecting to a hypothetical (and logging-required) Mumbai exit. For everyday browsing, calls, and streaming the difference is unnoticeable. For competitive gaming the difference matters and a VPN may not be the right tool for that use case.

How can Indian users verify our claims independently?

Five free tests from your own device — they take about fifteen minutes total. The framework is the same we publish for every OllaVPN page, with India-relevant additions for the local network environment.

Test 1 — Confirm the exit country. Connect to OllaVPN Free and open our what-is-my-IP tool. The country shown should be the exit country (typically Germany if you're routed via Frankfurt). If it shows India or your real ISP's IP, the tunnel didn't actually come up.

Test 2 — Confirm DNS goes through the tunnel. Use our DNS lookup tool while connected. The resolver IP should be the in-tunnel one (CGNAT 100.64.x.x range), not 8.8.8.8, 1.1.1.1, or your Jio/Airtel/BSNL DNS. Disconnect and repeat — you should see your ISP's resolver. The diff is your evidence.

Test 3 — Confirm ISP throttling is bypassed. If your ISP throttles a specific traffic type (Netflix, YouTube, streaming services), run a speed test on that service before connecting and again while connected. If the connected-speed is materially higher, the throttle was bypassed by the encryption.

Test 4 — Confirm no WebRTC leak. Open our WebRTC leak test. You should see only the exit's IPv4 address, never your real Indian IP. Indian ISPs typically do not assign IPv6 to consumer connections in 2026 so the IPv6 column will usually be empty.

Test 5 — Confirm the kill switch. Connect OllaVPN. Open a continuous ping in your terminal. Turn off mobile data or Wi-Fi. Pings should immediately fail. If they continue, the kill switch is broken (it isn't — but verify).
