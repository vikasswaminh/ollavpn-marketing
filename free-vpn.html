How can you verify our free VPN claims yourself?

Run five free tests from your own device in under fifteen minutes. Each test gives you a hard yes/no on a specific property of OllaVPN Free, using tools we host openly for anyone to use.

Claims on a marketing page are worth what the reader can verify independently. Every privacy property described above can be tested from your own browser or terminal — you do not have to take our word for any of it. We host the testing tools openly because we benefit when readers verify; the only operators who lose from a verification habit are the ones who quietly fail it.

Test 1 — DNS stays inside the tunnel. Connect OllaVPN Free. Open our DNS lookup tool in your browser and resolve any domain. The resolver IP returned should be the in-tunnel one — typically in the 100.64.0.0/10 CGNAT range. Disconnect, repeat. Now you should see your ISP's resolver instead. The diff between the two is your evidence that DNS goes through the tunnel while OllaVPN is active.

Test 2 — No WebRTC or IPv6 leak. Open our WebRTC leak test while connected. The page runs the same enumeration an attacker would: ICE candidate gathering, IPv6 address detection, secondary DNS leak checks. You should see only the chosen exit's IPv4 address. If any test reveals your real IP — public or private — the leak protection has failed and we want to hear about it at security@ollavpn.com.

Test 3 — Kill switch actually kills. Connect OllaVPN. Open a continuous ping in a terminal (ping 1.1.1.1 on Mac/Linux, ping -t 1.1.1.1 on Windows). Now disable your network interface — turn Wi-Fi off, unplug Ethernet, or toggle airplane mode. The ping should immediately fail and stay failed until you re-enable the network. If pings continue, that means your traffic switched silently to the underlying network — the kill switch is broken, walk away. (OllaVPN's doesn't, but verify.)

Test 4 — Apparent IP and country match the exit you picked. Open our what-is-my-IP tool. The IP shown should be the exit's IP, not your real one. The country should match the country you picked in the OllaVPN app. If the country is off (say you picked UK and the IP geo-locates to Germany), that is usually just IP-database drift — different geo-IP services can disagree on which subnet belongs to which country. If the IP is your real ISP's IP, the tunnel did not actually come up.

Test 5 — Speed cap is actually 10 Mbps on free. Run any speed test (fast.com, speedtest.net) while connected. The result should plateau around 9-10 Mbps download. Free tier honesty includes being honest about the cap; if the speed test shows substantially less than 10 Mbps, the bottleneck is the path between you and the chosen exit (try a closer one) rather than a stricter throttle.

If a candidate VPN — ours or anyone else's — fails any of these tests, that is hard evidence the marketing claims do not match the engineering. We hold ourselves to the same bar.

What OllaVPN Free deliberately does not ship

No iOS yet, no Linux desktop yet, no port forwarding on free, no streaming-service unblock promise, no in-tunnel ad blocker on free, no dedicated IP. Each non-feature has a reason — we explain them so you can decide if a different VPN is a better fit for your specific situation.

A free VPN guide should be honest about what is not in the product, not only what is. Things some readers might expect from "best free VPN" coverage that OllaVPN Free does not include, with the reason in each case:

No iOS app yet. iOS requires the Apple Developer Program ($99/year) and the Network Extension entitlement, which is on a roadmap behind our public launch. We did not want to ship a half-built iOS client just to claim the platform in a feature matrix. If you need iOS coverage today, Proton VPN Free, Windscribe Free, and hide.me Free all ship excellent iOS clients.

No Linux desktop client yet. Linux is a planned platform but is not in stage-1 scope. Power-user readers can use the standard wg-quick with a manually-fetched WireGuard config from the control plane in the meantime. Proton VPN's Linux client is the gold standard if you need a polished GUI today.

No port forwarding. Port forwarding is the single most reliable vector for hosting commercial services on residential IPs — and that pattern generates abuse complaints that the operator has to absorb at significant cost. We do not offer port forwarding. If a stable inbound port is central to your use case, a VPN that sells a dedicated port-forwarding feature is a better fit.

No split-tunneling. Per-app routing on macOS requires the NEAppProxyProvider path rather than the NEPacketTunnelProvider we use today; equivalent engineering work is needed on Windows. Both are on the roadmap but are not in the product today.

No multi-hop today. Sovereign Route — the cascade through a second entry-hop with PQC on both legs — is on the roadmap rather than in the product today, because the second-hop infrastructure cost is real and ongoing. Every user gets full PQC single-hop; the cryptographic strength of the tunnel is the same as the inner tunnel on Sovereign Route would be.

No in-tunnel ad blocking today. A resolver-level ad blocker (the Windscribe R.O.B.E.R.T. pattern) is on the roadmap. Blocklists have a high false-positive rate and we did not want to break sites for users who did not opt into the tradeoff. uBlock Origin in your browser handles the great majority of web ads in the meantime.

No promise of streaming-service compatibility. Netflix, Hulu, Disney+, and similar services actively block VPN IP ranges, and their blocklists rotate daily. We do not promise that any specific streaming service works through any specific OllaVPN exit at any specific time, because the promise would be wrong by next week. If reliable streaming-unlock is your primary need, a paid VPN with a dedicated streaming SKU is the honest recommendation.

No dedicated IP on any tier. A dedicated IP makes the operator's user-to-IP mapping persistent, which is a privacy regression we do not want to ship. If your use case requires a stable IP (some banking, some self-hosting), a paid VPN with a dedicated-IP SKU is more appropriate than OllaVPN.

When is a free VPN the right tool for your situation?

Free VPN is the right tool for public Wi-Fi protection, basic ISP throttling, casual geo-flexibility, and as a baseline privacy layer. It is not the right tool for guaranteed streaming unlock, hosting services on residential IPs, or replacing a corporate VPN. The decision framework below covers the common cases.

Free VPN is not a universally-correct tool — there are specific situations where it is the right answer, specific situations where a paid VPN is the better answer, and specific situations where no VPN solves the underlying problem. A decision framework:

Use a free VPN if you are on hotel, airport, or coffee-shop Wi-Fi and you are about to log into anything. Public Wi-Fi is the canonical "hostile network" scenario and the canonical use case where a free VPN — including OllaVPN Free — adds substantial protection over no VPN. HTTPS protects most web payloads in 2026, but DNS, SNI, and traffic-pattern metadata are still observable to other patrons on the network without a VPN.

Use a free VPN if your ISP is throttling specific traffic types (Twitch streams, BitTorrent, gaming traffic) and you want to test whether the throttle disappears when the traffic is encrypted. A free tier is sufficient to run the experiment; if the throttle goes away, you have the diagnostic. From there you can decide whether a paid VPN makes sense for sustained use.

Use a free VPN if you need to appear from a different country occasionally — to access a service that geo-locks by country, to test how a website renders for international users, to read a regional news site that blocks your home country. OllaVPN Free's "every country we serve" coverage is specifically designed for this use case.

Use a free VPN if you want a baseline privacy layer that makes bulk traffic capture less productive for adversaries. Free VPN with post-quantum encryption (which is OllaVPN's differentiator) substantially raises the cost-to-decrypt for the harvest-now-decrypt-later attack pattern. The defense is meaningful even if you are not a high-value target.

Pay for a paid VPN if you regularly need 4K streaming, sustained gigabit downloads, or many simultaneous devices. The free-tier speed cap and single-device limit exist because the infrastructure has real cost. OllaVPN is free and single-device by design; if your workload needs sustained gigabit or many devices at once, a paid VPN built for that is the honest recommendation.

Pay for a paid VPN with a streaming SKU if your primary use case is reliably watching a specific streaming catalog from outside its country. Free-tier VPN IP blocks are blocklisted faster than paid-tier rotating IPs. We do not pretend otherwise.

No VPN solves your problem if the threat you face is application-layer tracking (cookies, fingerprinting, account-based identity). A VPN replaces the ISP in the traffic-visibility role; it does not stop the websites themselves from tracking you. Use a privacy-respecting browser and tracker-blocking tools in addition.

No VPN solves your problem if what you actually need is source-anonymity against a global passive adversary. That is Tor's threat model. Use Tor — for that specific use case — and accept the latency cost.
